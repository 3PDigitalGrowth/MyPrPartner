The finding

The site is indexed, healthy and appearing in roughly 400 Australian searches a week. It converts almost none of them, because the searches it appears for have no page built to answer them.

That is a content gap, not a technical fault, and it is fixable with pages the SEO programme has already researched.

Section one

What three months of data actually says

The new site went live in early June and has now had three clean months in search. Google indexes all sixteen pages and shows the site for roughly 300 to 450 searches a week. The impression line is steady. The click line is flat on the floor.

Of the 19 Australian clicks, Google will name the search behind only six, and all six are the brand: my pr partner (4), crc public relations (1) and lyall mercer (1). The other thirteen sit in searches too rare for Google to disclose, which is itself a finding. They are scattered one-offs, not a cluster the site is winning.

GA4 tells the same story from the other side. Organic search delivered 115 sessions from 28 people over the quarter, and 26 sessions in August. Direct traffic, at 1,743 sessions, dwarfs it. That is the EDM programme and the team's own visits, not search.

Two things are worth holding onto. Average position has drifted from about 10 in May, when only brand searches matched, to the mid 30s in August, because Google is now testing the site against hundreds of non-brand queries at deep positions. That is the normal shape of a young site finding its topics. And 86 per cent of Australian impressions are on desktop, which fits an audience of principals, association executives and business owners researching at work.

Section two

Where the demand already is

The useful part of a Search Console read on a young site is not the clicks. It is the searches Google is already testing the site against, and how deep in the results it puts them. Each row below places the site's best position on a scale from first place to the bottom of page eight.

Three of those clusters carry real commercial value, have no page of their own, and sit in Australian search results the site can realistically enter. Those three are the recommendations in section four.

The brand cluster is the exception that proves the point: it is the only one where the site ranks in the top ten, and it is the only one producing clicks.

Section three

Four things holding the site back

01

The old WordPress URLs all return 404

Google still ranks the previous site's addresses and people still arrive on them. Over the quarter, dead paths collected 281 Australian impressions in Search Console and 292 page views in GA4. Several rank at positions 1 to 3 for their old searches. Every one of them lands on a 404, and the authority those pages built up is leaking away.

The only redirect in place today sends /programs/charity to /programs/business. Fixing this is a redirect map in next.config.mjs, roughly fifteen lines, and it is the fastest gain available on the whole site.

02

The articles page is empty

/articles is live, indexed and ranking, and there is nothing in it. Its only visible search demand is public relations assignment help at 66 impressions, which is university students, not clients.

Meanwhile the SEO programme has written fourteen articles, on crisis plans for schools, media training, press release writing, presentation skills, social media crisis management and internal comms. Every one sits in Google Docs in the shared Drive folder. Nothing has reached the site, so the topical depth that should be compounding underneath the program pages is parked. The weekly digest also counts blog engagement under /blog, a path this site does not have, so that panel reads zero until it is pointed at /articles.

03

The head term lands on a "coming soon" page

pr training is the biggest non-brand search the site appears for: 110 impressions at position 39, plus 26 for pr training services and 12 for pr media training. Google sends all of it to /courses, a 797-word page whose heading reads "Short, focused courses, coming to My PR Partner soon".

The programs, the masterclass and the trainer roster are the actual PR training offer, and none of them is what Google is matching. /courses needs to become a real page about PR training in Australia, with the four planned courses as one section inside it, or the head term keeps landing on a waiting list.

04

Google half-reads the site as a PR agency

Around 100 Australian impressions a quarter arrive from agency-intent searches: pr consulting (16), pr consultancy (11), pr partners (23), public relations partners (24), pr expert (8). Another 16 come from 10 to 1 pr, a different firm entirely.

The homepage title is right. The body copy talks about CRC's national PR company more than it talks about training organisations to run their own PR. A sharper opening section and title, something closer to "Online PR training for Australian schools, businesses and associations", pulls the site out of agency results it cannot win and into training results it can.

One smaller item alongside those four: www.myprpartner.com still redirects to the apex with a 307, a temporary redirect. Switching it to a 308 in Vercel's domain settings tells Google the move is permanent.

This is the largest pocket of commercial demand the site already touches, spread across five phrasings, and it has no home. Google forces it onto the Crisis Masterclass page, which covers a twelve-month crisis leadership programme and does not contain the words "media training" anywhere. That mismatch is why it sits at positions 49 to 75.

The Australian results for crisis media training australia are ThinkBox Media, Articulous, Crisis Shield, Communication & Media Manoeuvres, Saltwater Media and ICON: mid-sized trainers running a single service page each. A dedicated page with the depth of the existing program pages, around 2,000 words, and named former-journalist trainers can compete on that field. The masterclass page keeps the crisis communications course searches; this new page takes the media-facing ones.

What goes on it

  • Who it is for: chief executives, principals, association leaders, anyone who could face a camera in a bad week.
  • What a session covers: key messages under pressure, bridging difficult questions, television, radio and podcast formats, the first-hour holding statement, social media pile-ons.
  • How it is delivered: live online, recorded mock interviews, playback.
  • The trainers, drawn from the expert-trainers roster, with newsroom backgrounds stated.
  • The relationship to the Crisis Masterclass. This is the two-hour skill; the masterclass is the twelve-month capability.
  • A short FAQ and one enquiry form.

Linking

In from /crisis-masterclass, /about/expert-trainers, the homepage crisis section and /programs. Out to the masterclass and the three programs. Three articles already in the programme queue support it: crisis spokesperson training, media interview preparation, crisis media response procedures.

What good looks like: top 10 for crisis media training australia and crisis media management training within a quarter of publishing, and the first clicks Google attributes to a page other than the homepage.

The site is already on page one for six long-tail searches about affordable PR training for small firms, at positions 2.5 to 9, with no page targeting any of them. That demand currently goes to the homepage and the Business Program page, and neither uses the phrase "small business" once.

The Australian results for pr training for small business are unusually weak: a LinkedIn Learning topic page, a Gold Coast publicity coach, an SME-focused agency, a Melbourne Business School catalogue and a handful of state government training listings. Nobody owns this search. It is also the sharpest expression of the My PR Partner positioning, which is learning to do it yourself with senior advisers on call, instead of paying an agency retainer.

What goes on it

  • The honest cost comparison between a monthly subscription and a small agency retainer.
  • What an owner-operator actually needs in the first ninety days: a media release that gets opened, a local media list, a LinkedIn presence, a plan for the day something goes wrong.
  • How the Business Program's twelve months map onto that, reframed for a founder rather than a communications team.
  • What "on-call support" means in practice.
  • Three FAQs on time commitment, team size and cancellation, then a pricing pointer to the Business Program.

Linking

In from the homepage programs section, /programs and /programs/business. Out to /programs/business, the crisis checklist and the PR guide. Three queued articles support it: DIY PR for small businesses, why choose DIY PR training over a PR agency, and the PR agency alternative piece.

What good looks like: the six long-tail searches consolidate onto this page at positions 1 to 3, and it starts appearing for the broader small business pr and pr for small business australia searches where the site has no footprint at all today.

Schools are the programme's campaign priority for the rest of the year, and school visitors arriving from search behave differently from everyone else. The four organic sessions that landed on /programs/schools averaged 1,958 seconds on site, by a wide margin the longest of any page. Small numbers, but a clear signal about intent.

The Schools Program page already sits at positions 7 to 13 for school pr and public relations plan for schools with almost no supporting content around it. The Australian results for crisis communication plan for schools are nine American K-12 resources, ParentSquare, SchoolStatus, Verizon and ISM among them, plus one Australian trainer. There is no Australian guide with a template. That gap is the page.

What goes on it

  • The structure of a school crisis communication plan in the Australian context: roles on the crisis team, the approval chain, the first-hour holding statement.
  • Sequencing for staff, parents, students, the department or diocese, and media, then social media monitoring and the post-incident debrief.
  • The incidents schools actually face, worked through: a staff conduct allegation, a serious injury on an excursion, a parent campaign on Facebook.
  • A downloadable plan template, either free or behind an email address feeding the schools EDM list.
  • A clear line to the Schools Program and the Crisis Masterclass as the way to build the capability, not just the document.

Linking

In from /programs/schools, /crisis-masterclass, /resources/crisis-checklist and the homepage. Out to the Schools Program and the masterclass. Four queued articles support it, and the first of them, on why every school needs a plan before it needs one, is written and ready to publish.

What good looks like: page one for crisis communication plan for schools and school crisis communication plan in Australia, a template download feeding the schools list, and a lift in the Schools Program page's own rankings from the internal links.

The runner-up, for the record

Personal branding is the surprise in the data. Around 97 impressions a quarter arrive for personal branding coach australia and its variants, all landing on the dead trainers URL at positions 87 to 96, because one trainer's biography mentions it. The courses page lists a personal branding course as coming in 2026. When that course exists, a page for it starts warm. Until then it is a note, not a recommendation.

Section five

Order of work

  1. This week · 3P Digital

    Stop the leak

    The legacy redirect map into next.config.mjs, the 307 to 308 change on the www domain in Vercel, and pointing the weekly digest's blog metric at /articles instead of /blog. Roughly half a day, and it recovers traffic that already exists.

  2. Next · needs a decision

    Get the fourteen articles onto the site

    This needs a simple article route building, after which the SEO programme can commit posts straight into the site. Rewrite /courses into a real PR training page at the same time, with the four planned courses as a section inside it rather than the whole page.

  3. Then · in this order

    Build the three pages

    Crisis media training first, because it has the most demand waiting. The schools resource can jump ahead of small business if the EDM calendar wants it sooner.
